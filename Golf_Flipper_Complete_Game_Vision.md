# Golf Flipper — Complete Game Vision

## 1. Game Overview

**Golf Flipper** is a first-person golf-course restoration, management, and expansion simulator.

The player begins with one neglected property containing:

- A small three-hole golf course
- A tiny clubhouse
- A basic front desk
- A nearly empty pro shop
- A small parking lot
- No driving range
- Basic maintenance equipment
- Poor grass quality
- Dirty indoor spaces
- Limited cash
- A mortgage and operating bills
- A weak local reputation

The player personally restores and operates the property before gradually hiring workers and expanding the business.

The long-term goal is to transform the neglected three-hole course into a complete eighteen-hole golf destination with:

- A large clubhouse
- A full pro shop
- A driving range
- Practice greens
- Expanded parking
- Better landscaping
- More staff
- Memberships
- Tournaments
- Premium facilities
- Strong customer reviews

The game should focus heavily on **one property that continuously grows** rather than quickly moving between unrelated locations.

---

# 2. Core Player Fantasy

The player should feel like they personally built the entire golf business.

They should be able to look around the property and remember:

- Cleaning the original dirty clubhouse

- Manually booking the first walk-in customer
- Scanning the first product at the register
- Mowing the original three holes
- Expanding the first parking lot
- Building the first new hole
- Opening the driving range
- Expanding to eighteen holes

The game should combine:

- Property restoration
- Physical shop simulation
- Business management
- Customer service
- Golf-course maintenance
- Course design
- Interior decoration
- Long-term progression

# 3. Design Pillars

## 3.1 Physical Interactions

Important actions should happen physically inside the world.

Examples:

- Customers visibly drive into the parking lot.
- Customers exit their vehicles and walk into the clubhouse.
- Deliveries arrive in boxes.
- The player carries, opens, and empties the boxes.
- Products are manually placed on shelves.
- Customers carry products to the register.
- The player scans each product.
- Cards are physically swiped.
- Cash is physically accepted and placed into the drawer.
- Change is selected and handed back.
- Dirt appears on floors after customer traffic.
- Grass visibly grows and dries.
- The player physically mows and waters the course.
- The player cuts new cup locations and moves flagsticks.
- Furniture is picked up, rotated, and placed.

Menus should support the physical game, not replace it.

## 3.2 One Continuously Expanding Property

The original property should gradually become larger and more valuable.

Suggested progression:

1. Restore the original three-hole course.
2. Improve the clubhouse and parking.
3. Expand to six holes.
4. Expand to nine holes.
5. Build a driving range.
6. Expand the clubhouse again.
7. Add practice facilities.
8. Expand to twelve holes.
9. Expand to fifteen holes.
10. Complete the full eighteen-hole course.

## 3.3 Visible Cause and Effect

Every management decision should have a visible result.

Examples:

- Higher prices reduce demand.
- Cleaner facilities improve reviews.
- Better grass attracts more golfers.
- Empty shelves reduce pro-shop sales.
- Full parking causes customers to leave.
- More visitors create more dirt.
- Poor scheduling creates long waits.
- Bad weather changes bookings and maintenance needs.
- Better course design increases repeat visits.
- Hiring workers reduces the player’s workload but increases expenses.

1. A customer vehicle enters through the property entrance.

7. They walk toward the clubhouse.

- Deposit paid

## 7.1 Online Reservation Check-In

When the customer arrives:

1. They park.
2. They enter the clubhouse.
3. They approach the front desk.
4. They give their name.
5. The player searches for the reservation.
6. The player confirms group size and tee time.
7. The player adds rentals or products if requested.
8. The customer pays any remaining balance.
9. The player checks them in.
10. The player gives them a scorecard, rental equipment, cart key, or directions.
11. The customer proceeds toward the first tee, practice green, or range.

The player should be able to see whether a reservation is:

- Upcoming
- Checked in
- Late
- Cancelled
- Completed
- No-show
- Partially paid

4. Selects the number of holes.
5. Adds carts or walking.
6. Adds rental clubs.
7. Adds range access.
8. Applies a discount or membership benefit.

# 10. Physical Cash Register

The player can personally operate the register.

The full checkout sequence is:

1. Customer places products on the counter.
2. Player enters cashier mode.
3. Player picks up each product.
4. Player locates the barcode.
5. Player moves the barcode through the scanner.
6. The scanner activates.
7. The POS adds the product.
8. The scanned product is moved to the bagging area.
9. The customer selects card or cash.
10. The player completes the physical payment interaction.
11. A receipt prints.
12. Products are placed into a bag.
13. The player hands the bag to the customer.
14. The customer accepts it and leaves.

## 10.1 Card Payment

For card payments:

1. Customer presents a physical card.
2. The camera moves closer to the card reader.
3. The player controls the card.
4. Mouse movement physically moves the card down the swipe channel.
5. The terminal processes the payment.
6. The payment is approved or declined.
7. The camera returns to the normal cashier view.

## 10.2 Cash Payment

For cash payments:

1. Customer presents physical bills and coins.
2. Player accepts the money.
3. Cash drawer opens.
4. Player places received money into the drawer.
5. POS displays the required change.
6. Player selects bills and coins.
7. Player hands change to the customer.
8. Drawer closes.
9. Receipt prints.

The player should not simply press a “Complete Sale” button.

# 17. Weekly Hole and Pin Rotation

Hole locations should be changed regularly so players do not get bored and greens do not wear out in one area.

The player:

1. Chooses a new cup position.
2. Checks slope and turf wear.
3. Uses a cup cutter.
4. Removes the old cup.
5. Cuts the new hole.
6. Places the cup.
7. Moves the old turf plug.
8. Replaces the flagstick.
9. Updates the pin sheet.

Changing cup locations affects:

- Difficulty
- Variety
- Turf wear
- Customer reviews
- Tournament setup
- Replay value

Bad pin locations may create negative reviews.

Example:

> “Whoever put the pin on hole three today clearly woke up angry.”

# 29. Reputation and Demand

The number of customers should depend on the overall quality of the property.

## Positive Factors

- Clean clubhouse
- Healthy greens
- Fair pricing
- Friendly service
- Short waits
- Available parking
- Full pro-shop shelves
- Interesting course design
- Accurate reservations
- Fast checkout
- Attractive landscaping
- Useful facilities
- Good weather preparation

## Negative Factors

- Dirty bathrooms
- Empty shelves
- Long checkout lines
- Missing reservations
- Overbooked tee sheet
- Poor turf
- Unraked bunkers
- Full parking
- Broken equipment
- Bad signs
- Slow groups
- High prices
- Unhelpful workers
- Closed facilities

Higher demand creates more revenue, but also creates:

- More dirt
- More turf wear
- More queues
- More parking pressure
- More supply use
- More maintenance
- More customer complaints when operations fail

# 30. Suggested MVP

The first polished version should focus on making one small property feel complete.

## MVP Includes

- One three-hole course
- One small clubhouse
- One small parking lot
- One front desk
- One physical register
- One pro shop
- One storage room
- Online reservations
- Walk-in booking
- Check-in and payment
- Cash and card checkout
- Product deliveries
- Physical box opening
- Manual stocking
- Cleaning
- Mowing
- Watering
- Basic turf condition
- Weekly pin rotation
- Customer reviews
- Weather
- Bills
- Hourly labor
- Furniture placement
- Course-condition reputation

## Systems to Add Later

- Full eighteen-hole course
- Major tournaments
- Restaurant
- Advanced memberships
- Multiple large staff departments
- Multiple properties
- Large cart fleet
- Advanced lessons
- Large events

The starter three-hole property should already feel like a satisfying complete game before the larger expansions are added.

# 31. Final Late-Game Vision

In the late game, the player should be able to stand outside the original clubhouse and see:

- A full upgraded parking lot
- Customers arriving in vehicles
- Staff performing jobs
- A clean expanded clubhouse
- A busy front desk
- A fully stocked pro shop
- Multiple checkout counters
- Golfers checking in
- Customers shopping
- A functioning driving range
- Healthy greens
- Maintained bunkers
- Landscaped paths
- Golf carts moving
- A complete eighteen-hole course
- Strong reviews
- Profitable operations

The original starter property should still be recognizable underneath all the upgrades.

The player should always have something meaningful to:

- Clean
- Repair
- Stock
- Build
- Redesign
- Schedule
- Improve
- Maintain
- Pay
- Expand

That continuous improvement loop is the heart of Golf Flipper.
